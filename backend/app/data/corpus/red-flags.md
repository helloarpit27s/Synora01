---
title: Emergency red flags in primary-care triage
source: Internal triage policy, reviewed by clinical lead
tags: [triage, emergency, escalation]
---

Certain presentations bypass scoring entirely and are escalated to emergency care regardless of severity rating, duration, or patient history.

Chest pain or pressure accompanied by breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or pain radiating to the arm, jaw, or back is treated as a possible acute coronary event until assessed in person. Exertional onset raises concern further. The patient should not drive themselves.

Sudden facial drooping, one-sided weakness, slurred speech, or acute confusion is treated as a possible stroke. Time from onset determines treatment options, so the time symptoms were first noticed should be recorded and passed on.

A sudden severe headache described as the worst ever experienced, reaching peak intensity within a minute, requires emergency assessment even if it subsequently eases.

Blood in vomit or stool, black tarry stool, or a rigid abdomen with severe pain requires same-day assessment.

Breathlessness severe enough to prevent completing a sentence, fainting, or bleeding that cannot be controlled with direct pressure all require emergency services.

Triage tooling must never soften, delay, or rationalise these presentations. An automated system's role is to recognise them and step out of the way.
